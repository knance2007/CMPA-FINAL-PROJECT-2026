This retrospective reflects on my project process using the three key questions from PMUPM Chapter 7: what went well, what went wrong, and what I would do differently. This project involved building a two‑page fictional law firm website, along with supporting documents such as a scope statement, project plan, and this retrospective.

### What Went Well
One of the strongest parts of this project was how quickly the structure came together once I defined the scope. Creating the home page and about page early on gave me a clear foundation to build from, and sticking to semantic HTML helped keep everything organized and readable.

The visual elements also came together nicely. The full‑width banner on the home page and the three‑image layout on the About page made the site feel more polished and intentional. Using Flexbox made alignment easier and gave the layout a clean, modern feel.

Another win was maintaining documentation throughout the process. Writing the scope statement, plan, and retrospective alongside the build helped me stay aligned with my goals and avoid drifting into unnecessary features. This is something PMUPM emphasizes, and I could feel how much smoother the project went because of it.

### What Went Wrong
Most of the issues I ran into were small but surprisingly time‑consuming. Image paths caused more trouble than expected — especially dealing with capitalization, file extensions, and filenames with spaces. These weren’t major problems, but they slowed me down and reminded me how sensitive web development can be to tiny details.

I also underestimated how long it would take to fine‑tune spacing, sizing, and layout. Even with Flexbox, I found myself adjusting widths, margins, and object‑fit values more than I planned. It wasn’t “wrong,” but it did highlight that design work often requires more iteration than expected.

### What I’d Do Differently
If I were starting this project again, I would set up a more consistent file‑naming system from the beginning. Using lowercase letters, avoiding spaces, and keeping extensions predictable would have prevented most of the image issues I ran into.

I would also sketch out the layout before jumping into the CSS. Even a simple wireframe would have helped me make styling decisions faster and reduced the amount of trial‑and‑error.

From a project‑management perspective, I’d break my tasks into smaller, more trackable pieces. PMUPM Chapter 7 emphasizes learning from the process, and I can see how more granular task planning would have made my workflow smoother and easier to estimate. I’ll carry that forward into future coursework and projects.
